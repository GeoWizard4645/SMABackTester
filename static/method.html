1. The big picture

Traders watch the 200-day moving average. If enough of them buy when price falls to it and sell when price rises to it, it could become a genuine support/resistance level purely because everyone believes in it, a self-fulfilling prophecy. This lab turns that idea into three testable claims:

#ClaimHow it is tested
H1Price reacts to the target line more than to a line nobody watches.Identical event rules for the target and one or more control lines (arbitrary window lengths).
H2“Reacts” means price turns away from the line instead of slicing through it.Bounce rate and abnormal forward return, direction-adjusted.
H3The effect is larger in the modern algorithmic / retail era.Difference-in-differences between a late era and an early era (you choose both).

Why a control? Any moving average will look like support in hindsight, because a smoothed line sits near where price has been. Only the difference between the target and a comparable arbitrary line says anything about coordination. The null hypothesis is: the target behaves exactly like the control.

3. Indicators

Notation: for bar $t$, $O_t,H_t,L_t,C_t$ are open, high, low, close; $N$ is the line's window.

Moving average (three kinds)

$$\text{SMA}_t=\frac1N\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}C_{t-i}\qquad \text{EMA}_t=\alpha C_t+(1-\alpha)\text{EMA}_{t-1},\ \alpha=\tfrac{2}{N+1}\qquad \text{WMA}_t=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(N-i)\,C_{t-i}}{N(N+1)/2}$$

All include the current bar. A line is only used once $N$ bars exist (so the SMA/WMA need $N$ bars; the EMA is started with the first close and trusted after $N$).

True range and ATR (Wilder, default 14)

$$\text{TR}_t=\max\big(H_t-L_t,\ |H_t-C_{t-1}|,\ |L_t-C_{t-1}|\big),\qquad \text{ATR}_t=\text{ATR}_{t-1}+\tfrac1{14}(\text{TR}_t-\text{ATR}_{t-1})$$

ATR measures how far price typically moves in a day. Measuring distances in ATRs means “close to the line” means the same thing in calm and in wild markets.

Distance and range expansion

$$d_t=\frac{C_t-\text{MA}_t}{\text{MA}_t},\quad d^{\text{ATR}}_t=\frac{C_t-\text{MA}_t}{\text{ATR}_t},\quad \rho^{\text{TR}}_t=\frac{\text{TR}_t}{\frac1{20}\sum_{i=1}^{20}\text{TR}_{t-i}}$$

$\rho^{\text{TR}}$ compares the event day's range with the previous 20 days (the event is excluded from its own baseline). A value of 1 is a normal day; the lab also reports the ATR version, which moves slowly and stays near 1.

4. What counts as a “touch”

The tolerance band around the line is $[\text{MA}_t-b\,\text{ATR}_t,\ \text{MA}_t+b\,\text{ATR}_t]$ with $b=0.5$ by default (set “Band” in the sidebar). A touch is an approach from a definite side: price must have spent the previous $m=5$ closes on one side of the line first.

$$\text{Support test: }\ C_{t-i}\gt \text{MA}_{t-i}\ (i=1..m)\ \wedge\ L_t\le \text{MA}_t+b\,\text{ATR}_t$$ $$\text{Resistance test: }\ C_{t-i}\lt \text{MA}_{t-i}\ (i=1..m)\ \wedge\ H_t\ge \text{MA}_t-b\,\text{ATR}_t$$

Let $s_t=+1$ for a support test and $-1$ for a resistance test.

Try it: is this bar a touch?

De-clustering

Price often hugs a line for days, producing near-identical events. Candidates are processed in time order and accepted only if $i-i_{\text{last accepted}}>R$ ($R=10$ bars by default; set “De-cluster”). This also keeps the forward windows of different events from overlapping, so observations are closer to independent.

5. Measuring the reaction

All outcomes start from the close of the event day, $C_t$, so nothing about that day's own result leaks into the entry.

Forward return and the unconditional benchmark

$$R_{t\to t+k}=\frac{C_{t+k}-C_t}{C_t}\qquad \mu_k(e)=\underset{s\in e}{\text{mean}}\ R_{s\to s+k}$$

$\mu_k(e)$ is the average $k$-bar return over all bars of the same era $e$, for the same asset. It removes drift: after a support test a positive return proves little in a bull market. The target and every control share one benchmark.

Direction-adjusted abnormal return (CAR)

$$\text{CAR}_k=s_t\cdot\big(R_{t\to t+k}-\mu_k(e_t)\big)$$

Multiplying by $s_t$ flips resistance tests so that positive always means “price reacted the way a support/resistance level predicts”; the two directions can then be pooled.

Bounce

$$\text{Support: }\ \mathbb 1\Big[C_{t+k}\gt C_t\ \wedge\ C_u\ge \text{MA}_u-\beta\,\text{ATR}_t\ \ \forall u\in[t,t+k]\Big]$$

Price must finish higher and never close more than $\beta=1.5$ ATR through the line (frozen ATR from the event day; the line itself moves). Resistance is the mirror image. If the window runs past the end of the data the bounce is missing, never counted as a failure.

Event-study path

$$\text{AR}_j=\frac{C_{t+j}}{C_{t-5}}-1-\mu^{\text{path}}_j(e),\qquad j=-5,\dots,10$$

Plotted as the average across events with a 95% band $\bar x\pm1.96\,\text{SE}$.

6. Confidence intervals

Bounce rates use the Wilson score interval, which behaves well for small samples and extreme rates ($\hat p=x/n$, $z=1.96$):

$$\frac{\hat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}\ \pm\ z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}}$$

Mean CAR uses a Student-$t$ interval, $\bar x\pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,s/\sqrt n$.

7. Target vs control

For each era and metric, with $\Delta=\bar y_{\text{target}}-\bar y_{\text{control}}$ (positive is the direction the hypothesis predicts), the lab reports Student's two-sample $t$, Welch's $t$ (unequal variances), the Mann-Whitney $U$ test (rank-based), and a year-cluster bootstrap.

Why the first three are not enough. They assume the two samples are independent. They are not: both lines are tested on the same price path and share many events. That makes them conservative, on simulated data with no effect they reject only about 1% of the time at a nominal 5%. Events within a year are also dependent. The cluster bootstrap accounts for both, so it is the p-value to trust.

8. The year-cluster bootstrap

  1. Group events by calendar year; a year keeps both lines' events together.
  2. Resample whole years with replacement, $B$ times (the “Bootstrap reps” setting).
  3. For each replicate compute $\Delta^*$.
  4. CI: the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of $\Delta^*$.
  5. p-value, recentred on the observed difference $\hat\Delta$: $\displaystyle p=\frac{1+\#\{|\Delta^*-\hat\Delta|\ge|\hat\Delta|\}}{B+1}$.

With fewer than 5 distinct years the bootstrap is meaningless, so the lab reports n/a instead of a number. Calibrated on 60 simulated no-effect histories it rejects about 5–9% of the time at a nominal 5%: slightly liberal when an era has few years.

9. Eras and “did the effect grow?”

You can define any number of eras (2–8) by the last year of each, split each asset's history into equal slices, or use the default regimes (1950–1990, 1991–2007, 2008+) for assets with long histories. An era is just a label on each date; every benchmark is computed within the era.

The estimand for H3 is a difference-in-differences between the early era you pick and the late era you pick:

$$\text{DiD}=\underbrace{(\bar y_{\text{target}}-\bar y_{\text{control}})_{\text{late}}}_{\Delta_{\text{late}}}-\underbrace{(\bar y_{\text{target}}-\bar y_{\text{control}})_{\text{early}}}_{\Delta_{\text{early}}}$$

Subtracting the control removes anything that affects all lines equally in an era (drift, volatility, how far price wanders from its averages). Two estimates are shown:

  • Stratified cluster bootstrap: years resampled independently inside the early and the late era; $\text{DiD}^*=\Delta^*_{\text{late}}-\Delta^*_{\text{early}}$.
  • Interaction regression on the pooled events of both lines (shown for three eras; extra eras get their own dummies): $$y=\beta_0+\beta_1T+\beta_2E_2+\beta_3E_3+\beta_4(T E_2)+\beta_5(T E_3)+\varepsilon$$ $T=1$ for the target line. The interaction coefficient for the late era is the DiD. Standard errors are cluster-robust by calendar year (CR1): $$\hat V=\frac{G}{G-1}\frac{N-1}{N-K}(X^\top X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_g X_g^\top\hat u_g\hat u_g^\top X_g\Big)(X^\top X)^{-1}$$ with $p$-values from $t_{G-1}$.

10. The line scan: is the target special?

Comparing against one or two hand-picked controls is arbitrary. The Line scan tab runs the same event study for every line length in a range (e.g. 20 to 400) and asks where your target lands among them. If the 200 were a genuine focal point it should stand out from its neighbours; if it sits in the middle of the pack, any line would have looked as good.

The empirical one-sided p-value is $\dfrac{1+\#\{\text{other lines with metric}\ge\text{target}\}}{M+1}$ over the $M$ other lines. Lines within a few bars of the target are excluded because they share almost all their events; even so, nearby lines are strongly correlated, so treat this as a guide rather than a formal test.

12. Limits and honest caveats

  • Multiple testing. Assets × controls × horizons × eras × metrics is dozens of p-values with no correction. About 1 in 20 will cross 0.05 by luck. A hit at one horizon, in one era, on one asset is weak evidence.
  • Few events. Eras often contain tens of events per line, so intervals are wide and power for small effects is low.
  • Short histories. Newer assets (crypto, recent listings) have few calendar years per era. The cluster methods are unreliable below ~10 years per era and are switched off below 5.
  • Old data. The S&P 500 index before ~1962 has no true intraday high/low, so ATR and touches there are close-to-close approximations.
  • 24/7 markets. For crypto an $N$-bar line spans $N$ calendar days and horizons are calendar days.
  • Free data. Prices come from Yahoo Finance (split/dividend adjusted); no survivorship or error checking beyond basic cleaning.
  • One asset at a time. A null on one index does not rule out effects elsewhere, and a positive result would not prove the mechanism is trader coordination.
  • Not advice. This is a research tool; it ignores costs, slippage and position sizing.

13. Glossary

ATRAverage True Range: typical daily price movement.
Abnormal returnReturn minus what an average day of the same era delivered.
BarOne daily candle (a calendar day for crypto).
BootstrapRe-running a statistic on resampled data to see how much it varies by chance.
CARCumulative abnormal return, direction-adjusted so positive = reacted as S/R predicts.
ClusterA group of dependent observations (here, a calendar year) resampled as a unit.
De-clusteringDropping events too soon after another, so they do not overlap.
DiDDifference-in-differences: how much the target-minus-control gap changed between two eras.
EraA calendar-year range treated as one market regime.
Wilson intervalA confidence interval for a proportion that stays sensible for small samples.
